Every mechanism the team builds — the intake, the shooter, the drivetrain — goes through the same process: design it, build it, test it, and when something needs to change, figure out exactly what kind of change it is. This guide walks through that process end to end, and the four words that keep it straight along the way: Assembly, Build, Part, and Revision.

The four words, for reference

The vocabulary the story above runs on.

A

Assembly

A mechanism that does one job. A new one exists only when the whole mechanism is genuinely different — a different form, fit, or function, not just a different part.

Space City Intake and Manor Intake are two Assemblies because they're different machines doing a related job.

R

Revision

The same Assembly, reworked significantly. Rare — most Assemblies are only ever on their first Revision and never need a second.

A ground-up rework of Space City Intake that's still doing the intake's job would be a Revision, not a new Assembly.

B

Build

The specific combination of parts actually put together. Same Assembly, same Revision, but a different set of parts.

Build 1 and Build 2 of Space City Intake differ in exactly one part: the bash bar.

P

Part

One physical piece. Carried forward unchanged across Builds, or redesigned when a Build needs something different.

The side plates carried forward untouched. The bash bar got a new version.

Who does what

The same mechanism passes through a few hands on its way to the field.

Designing it

CAD students design the mechanism and its parts, and make the first call on whether what they're building is a tweak to an existing Build, a new Build, or something bigger.

Making it real

The shop machines, prints, and orders whatever the current Build's parts list calls for.

Putting it together

The assembly and pit crew builds it, keeps it running at competitions, and decides whether a part carries forward unchanged or needs to be redesigned for the next Build.

The bigger calls

Starting a new Revision or a new Assembly is open to any student — but because they're harder to undo than picking a Build, they're worth a second look or a quick check with a mentor before committing.

How to tell what's current

At any competition, a lot happens fast. These four states are how the shop and the pit crew agree on what to trust right now.

What the tool does for you

You design in OnShape and give every part a name and a material. The rest is the tool's job, so you don't type part numbers, Revision letters or file names.

Update from CAD reads your assembly. A part with no number gets the next free one inside your Assembly's block (Assembly 1100 uses 1101 to 1199), and Revision A. Machinery, powdercoat and thickness are filled in for you where the tool can tell, and you can change any of them.

Tick the rows and Save, once. The tool writes the number back into OnShape, makes the Part, and adds it to your Build. A moment later each Part has a picture, a STEP file and a DXF of its flat side (a tube gets its top, right and front sides), and its picture shows in the table.

A new Build either reuses the Parts of the last one that was cut, or makes everything new. If a reused part's design changed, the tool notices and gives it the next Revision on its own. If you need more of a part than the Build already holds, it adds a row to make the extra ones.

When a Build is cut, the tool puts each part's Revision into OnShape and saves the version, and the Build keeps a picture of the whole Assembly.

Parts that come from another OnShape document (a vendor part) are bought, not made. They aren't in the Designer, and they're listed under Off-the-shelf on the Assembly page.

Keeping it all on record

None of this depends on anyone's memory. As each of these moments happens — a design is finished, a Build is ready, a part carries forward or gets redesigned — it gets written down as it happens, so the history behind every Build is still there months later.

You don't need to know how that's recorded to follow this process. Just that it is — and that whatever you're looking at reflects a real decision somebody already made, not a guess.

A few things worth knowing

Do I need permission to start a Build or a Revision?
No — any student can do either. A Revision is the bigger decision of the two, so take a second look before you commit to it.
Not sure which Build to grab?
Take the one marked Active. There's always exactly one per Revision.
A part changed a little — does that make it a new version?
If the shape actually changed, yes, and the tool notices and gives the part its next Revision when you Update from CAD. If it's the same as what's already there, no — it just carries forward into whichever Build needs it.
I changed something in OnShape and the tool doesn't show it.
It shows new parts, a changed quantity or material, and a changed shape. Names, descriptions and part numbers you type in OnShape don't come across, and neither do edits made in a different OnShape configuration than your Assembly follows. If it's none of those, ask a mentor to check which configuration the Assembly uses.
